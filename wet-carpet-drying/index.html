Wet Carpet Drying: What The Villages Homeowners Should Know

A wet carpet is hiding a lot more water than you can see. Up top the fibers may seem barely damp, yet the pad has filled up the way a sponge does and the subfloor under it has gotten wet as well. A shop vacuum and a stack of towels only deal with the surface. For The Villages homes, our crew handles the extraction, meters the carpet, pad and subfloor, and runs drying gear that gets to the bottom layer.

Whether the carpet can be saved depends on two things, how clean the water was and how long it has been wet. Carpet soaked by a clean supply line can usually be dried in place if we get to it quickly. Water from an appliance counts as gray, so the pad is frequently removed while the carpet is cleaned and treated. After sewage or flood water from outdoors, neither the carpet nor the pad stays.

Signs to Look For

  • Dampness that spreads outward on its own, well after the leak was stopped
  • Soft or squeaky subfloor beneath the area of carpet that was soaked
  • Swelling in the baseboards, or bubbled paint just above them
  • Ripples or loose spots where the carpet has let go of the tack strip
  • A rust colored line at the wall edge, a sign of wet tack strip
  • Carpet that still feels cold and clammy to bare feet days after the spill
What We Do

Our Wet Carpet Drying Process

1

Map the Wet Area

We trace how far the water traveled with moisture meters and thermal imaging, including under walls and into closets. Then we determine which category of water it is, as that tells us what is safe to keep.

2

Extract the Water

With weighted extractors, carpet and the pad beneath it are pressed and emptied in one pass. A wand by itself leaves much more behind, so this step cuts the drying time afterward.

3

Dry Every Layer

The carpet is either pulled back so the pad can go, or left down with air blown beneath it, whichever the job needs. Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on until the subfloor and the carpet each test dry.

4

Clean and Reinstall

Where needed, an antimicrobial goes on the subfloor and carpet, then new pad is laid if the old was removed, and the carpet is stretched back on its tack strip and cleaned.

What You Cannot See

The Damage You Do Not See

Subfloor Damage

Particleboard and OSB under carpet swell and crumble when they stay wet. Squeaks, soft spots and raised seams show up weeks after the spill is forgotten.

Soaked Pad Underneath

Pad can hold many times its weight in water. It keeps feeding moisture into the carpet above and the floor below long after the surface feels dry.

Mold on the Backing

Mold tends to start underneath, on the carpet back, because that side stays damp and dark. The odor arrives a long time ahead of any visible sign on top.

Clean Water Turns Dirty

Even clean water goes bad the longer it sits. As it sits in carpet and pad it picks up soil and bacteria, so a carpet that could have been dried may have to be removed.

If Nothing Is Done

How the Damage Progresses

First hours

Water Spreads Sideways

Under the surface, the pad pulls water outward, well beyond what looks wet. The tack strip starts taking on water as it creeps below the walls toward the room next door.

24 to 48 hours

Smell and Mold Start

That sour wet carpet odor shows up as bacteria multiply in the damp pad. Dust held down in the fibers and the carpet backing both give mold a place to begin.

Days 3 through 7

The Carpet Breaks Down

As the latex bonding the backing gives out, the carpet delaminates and starts to ripple. The subfloor swells and the tack strip nails begin to rust.

After a week

Too Late to Save

By now the carpet and pad usually have to come out. Often mold has spread to the subfloor and the base of the drywall, which adds to the job.
